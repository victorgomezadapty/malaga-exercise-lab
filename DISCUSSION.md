# Discussion and open questions

This note places the primary results next to a current literature search (November 2026) and lists
what can still be investigated in this open table. It is not a systematic review. Every source below
was checked by resolving its DOI (and, where the article is open, by reading the abstract and the
relevant results or discussion). Paper text, tables and figures are cited only, not reused. This
project does not recommend a formula for training or clinical use.

## How the present results sit with recent work

The honest headline in this table is not the near-zero mean bias of Tanaka (2001) and Gellish
(2007). It is the individual error: mean absolute error of 7.1 bpm for those two formulas and
8.2 bpm for 220 minus age, with more than 10 bpm of error in 26.3%, 26.7% and 30.5% of first adult
tests, respectively (n = 735).

That pattern is consistent with recent external checks. Martin, Lindsey, Gerrity and Ambegaonkar
(2025) compared seven age-based maximal heart rate equations in 230 healthy adults (174 men and
56 women; mean age 38.5 years, range 18 to 68) who completed maximal graded exercise tests between
2019 and 2024. Mean biases were small (about -3 to +6 bpm), but the 95% limits of agreement were
wide (about 18 to 24 bpm either side of the mean). Arena, Tanaka and Gellish had the lowest mean
absolute error and RMSE; none of the equations showed high accuracy at the individual level.
Cardiorespiratory fitness had only a limited influence on prediction error (and only a small
association in men). They conclude that future work should try more individualized models, while
noting that adjusting for fitness alone may not improve accuracy much in healthy adults, and that
their results are exploratory.

Kasiak, Wiecha, Cieslinski, Takken, Lach, Lewandowski and colleagues (2023) externally validated
prediction models in 4,043 runners and 1,026 cyclists. Nine of 13 models differed significantly
from measured maximal heart rate; RMSE was 9.1 to 10.5 bpm, close to the 9.2 to 10.5 bpm found
here. They judged cardiopulmonary exercise testing preferable to a predicted maximum, and they
flagged a clear sample limit: women were only 15.01% of participants, with little racial diversity.
The primary sample here has the same problem (102 women of 735).

Lach, Wiecha, Sliz, Price, Zaborski, Cieslinski and colleagues (2021), in 3,374 mostly athletic
clinic clients, found that age alone explains maximal heart rate only to a limited degree, and
that adding body composition, VO2max, sex and testing modality improved prediction only slightly
and was "unlikely to be useful in clinical practice." Their multivariate model had R-squared 0.22
and MAE 7.04 bpm. Tanaka was the second-best published formula; 220 minus age produced mean errors
of up to 9 bpm. That MAE is in the same range as the 7.1 to 8.2 bpm reported here.

The age pattern of 220 minus age in this table (bias 8.5 bpm at 18 to 21 years, falling to
-4.1 bpm at 45 and over) matches the long-standing critique in Tanaka, Monahan and Seals (2001)
and Gellish, Goslin, Olson, McDonald, Russi and Moudgil (2007). Almaadawy, Uretsky, Krittanawong
and Birnbaum (2024), in a review of target heart rate formulas for exercise stress testing,
likewise note that the Fox equation tends to overestimate predicted maximal heart rate in young
adults and underestimate it in older adults, that a rigid 85% cutoff of age-predicted maximal
heart rate is a poor marker of exertion, and that validation in people aged 60 and over remains
weak. The oldest band in the present primary analysis (45 and over) has only 63 people, so this
table does not close that gap.

## What is distinctive here, and what is not

The companion papers on this deposit model the dynamics of heart rate and oxygen uptake, and
methods for heart rate recovery. They are cited only. This re-analysis asks a different question:
how far three cited formulas, and zones at 70% and 85% of maximal heart rate, depart from the
observed effort-phase peak.

The distinctive number is zone discordance. On average, 14.7% of effort-phase time would change
zone label with 220 minus age, 12.4% with Tanaka and 12.3% with Gellish. That is not a ventilatory
threshold analysis, and it is not a prescription. It only says that swapping a formula for the
observed peak moves a non-trivial share of effort time across the 70% and 85% lines in this table.

Recent work on training zones makes the same kind of warning from a different angle. Meixner,
Filipas, Holmberg and Sperlich (2025) compared common Zone 2 markers in 50 cyclists and found
substantial individual variability (coefficients of variation from 6% to 29%). Ventilatory
threshold 1 and maximal fat oxidation aligned more closely with each other, while fixed percentages
of maximal heart rate showed wide individual differences. Whether formula-based 70% and 85% zones
also miss metabolic thresholds in this same Malaga table is still an open question: thresholds
were not computed here, by design.

A further limit of scope: this deposit is mostly athletic treadmill tests. Han, Choi, Kim and
colleagues (2022) found that age-predicted maximal heart rate differed significantly from the
measured peak in 827 patients with heart or lung disease, especially in those taking beta-blockers.
This table cannot answer that clinical question.

## What can still be investigated

These questions are grounded in the limits of the present analysis and in gaps that the cited
papers also name. They are not a work plan already executed.

1. Threshold concordance on this same table. Compare a pre-specified ventilatory or lactate
   threshold rule, or heart rate reserve, with 70% and 85% of both the observed peak and each
   formula. Meixner et al. (2025) show why the answer is not obvious. This project left that
   comparison out on purpose.
2. Sex-specific accuracy, with enough women. Kasiak et al. (2023) had only 15% women; this primary
   sample has 102 of 735. Sex-specific formulas remain under-tested here.
3. People under 18. The 122 first tests under 18 were kept descriptive and were not pooled into
   the primary family. A clearly labelled paediatric analysis is still to be done, not implied.
4. Older adults, especially 60 and over. Almaadawy et al. (2024) note that validation in that
   group is still weak. The present 45-and-over band is small (n = 63) and stops at age 63.0.
5. Whether fitness, body composition or modality improves prediction by a practically useful
   margin. Lach et al. (2021) and Martin et al. (2025) suggest the gain may be small. It can still
   be tested on this table if gas-data quality allows, with the 11 tests that lack VO2 and VCO2
   handled as a pre-specified exclusion, not a silent fix.
6. Repeat-test stability of the formula error in the 116 people who have more than one test.
   The primary analysis used only the first adult test.
7. A clearer maximal-effort rule (for example a respiratory exchange ratio or a heart-rate
   plateau) versus the effort-phase peak used here. Some of the apparent formula error may be a
   submaximal test rather than a bad equation (Martin et al., 2025, also discuss test criteria).
8. Clinical and medicated populations, including beta-blockers (Han et al., 2022). This athletic
   deposit does not substitute for that work.

None of these items requires a new formula as a first step, and none of them is training advice.
